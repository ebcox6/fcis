The Three Protections

Run any AI decision in your classroom or school through three questions.

Joy

Does this use of AI give students more time for the parts of learning worth doing, or take those parts away?

Integrity

Can the student still show their own thinking and be honest about how AI was used?

Safety

Do we know where student data goes, and is everyone in the room protected?

All three deep dives

Each room goes deep on one protection. Here are all three: the case, the questions to discuss, and three moves you can use.

Same goal, opposite moves

On September 2, 2026, New York City Public Schools paused student-facing generative AI for one year from 2-K through 8th grade and banned AI companion chatbots in every grade. Teachers kept approved AI for planning and operations, but not for grading, behavior monitoring, or placement. The mayor’s reason was human connection. The same school year, 64% of educators who used AI said it gave them more time for the parts of teaching that matter most.

Sources: NYC Public Schools announcement, as reported by K-12 Dive, Sept. 2, 2026. Walton Family Foundation and Gallup educator survey, released Oct. 14, 2026 (13% “a lot more time” plus 51% “somewhat more time”).

Discuss

  • Which of the Three Protections does the pause guard? Is it only one?
  • What do students lose when they never practice AI with an adult nearby?
  • How would you protect the same human connection without a ban?

Three moves

  1. Think first, then AI. Students draft or solve on their own first, then AI questions or critiques their thinking.In an MIT Media Lab study, writers who started without AI and then revised with it showed more ownership (Kosmyna et al., June 2025 preprint, 54 adult participants).
  2. Human-only zones. Name where AI stays out: discussion, peer feedback, first creative attempts.New York drew this line for teachers too.
  3. Reinvest saved time. Decide which human thing the saved time goes to: a conference, a small group, a call home.Arkansas teachers turned saved time into daily intervention blocks (Gallup and Walton Family Foundation research, via KUAF, Nov. 18, 2025).

When the score becomes the verdict

In November 2024, an Adelphi University freshman’s essay, written with a program tutor, got a zero after Turnitin reported it as fully AI-written. His appeal went to the same official who made the finding, and two detectors that scored it human-written were never considered. In January 2026, a New York court annulled the finding and ordered his record expunged. The court faulted the process, not the tool. It also noted that courts give private schools broad discretion and check whether a school followed its own rules.

In K-12: in May 2026, a Palo Alto family filed a federal complaint after their sophomore’s essay was flagged 76% AI. The complaint argues the score was treated as decisive. These are allegations, not findings.

Sources: Matter of Newby v Adelphi Univ., 2026 NY Slip Op 26021 (Jan. 28, 2026). The Almanac / Palo Alto Online, May 11, 2026.

Discuss

  • Name the moment a person should have stepped in before the zero.
  • If not a score, what would convince you a student did the work?
  • What would a fair AI-integrity process look like at your school?

Three moves

  1. Make AI use visible. State the allowed AI use on every assignment, and ask students to note how they used it.Hingham High held up in court because its rules were clear before anything went wrong (Harris v. Adams, D. Mass., Nov. 20, 2024).
  2. Collect the process. Drafts, version history, and a two-minute “walk me through it” conversation.Process evidence is something a parent, an appeal, or a court can follow.
  3. A score is a question. A detector flag starts a conversation and never decides a grade. Appeals go to someone new.That is exactly where Adelphi failed.

Did the AI fail, or did it work?

On December 9, 2025, an AI weapons-detection camera at Lawton Chiles Middle School in Oviedo flagged a student holding something like a rifle. A reviewer at the company confirmed the alert, the school locked down, and police responded. It was a clarinet, carried by a student in camo for a themed dress-up day. Families got a quick message, but not the context. The district said the system worked as intended. Nationally, only 4% of parents said their school had told them about the risks and penalties of sharing sexually explicit deepfakes.

Sources: WKMG News 6 (ClickOrlando), Dec. 18, 2025. Center for Democracy & Technology, “In Deep Trouble,” Sept. 2024.

Discuss

  • Did it fail or work? Make the case both ways.
  • Name every person in the chain. Who could have changed the outcome?
  • What would you tell families within the hour, and who decides?

Three moves

  1. Know your tools’ data. For each AI tool students use: what data goes in, where it lives, and who reviews it.The PowerSchool breach exposed Social Security numbers, grades, and medical information at schools across the country (TechCrunch, 2025).
  2. Write the deepfake rule. Name AI-generated sexual images and impersonation in your harassment policy, and teach it.Hingham’s AI policy covered only cheating, so no rule applied when a middle schooler made a fake nude of a classmate (The Boston Globe, Apr. 9, 2026).
  3. Tell families early. Keep an AI-incident message ready: what happened, what the tool did, who decided.In Oviedo, the missing context became the story.
